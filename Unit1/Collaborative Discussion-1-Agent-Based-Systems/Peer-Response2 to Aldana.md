
Thank you, Aldana. I agree that governance and accountability must develop alongside increasing agent autonomy. Your question about where organizations should draw the boundary between delegation and human approval is particularly important.

I think this boundary should be determined through a risk-based approach rather than by the technical capability of the agent alone. Decisions that are low-impact, reversible, routine, and governed by clearly defined rules could reasonably be delegated fully to agents. For example, an agent might autonomously prioritize support requests, retrieve information, or optimize routine workflows within predefined limits.

In contrast, human approval should remain mandatory where decisions have significant financial, legal, ethical, safety, or security consequences, particularly when the outcome is difficult to reverse. This would include actions such as approving large financial transactions, modifying critical infrastructure, disclosing sensitive information, or making decisions that substantially affect individuals. Jennings (2000) highlights that interactions between autonomous agents can produce complex system-level behaviour, which makes accountability particularly important in such situations.

I would therefore view autonomy as a graduated spectrum rather than an all-or-nothing choice. Between full automation and mandatory human approval, organizations could allow agents to act autonomously within defined thresholds while escalating exceptional, uncertain, or high-risk cases to humans. Continuous monitoring, auditability, and override mechanisms would then provide additional safeguards.

This approach also reflects Russell’s (2019) argument that increasingly capable systems should remain aligned with human objectives. Ultimately, the question should not simply be “Can the agent make this decision?” but “What would be the consequence if the agent makes the wrong decision, and who remains accountable?”
