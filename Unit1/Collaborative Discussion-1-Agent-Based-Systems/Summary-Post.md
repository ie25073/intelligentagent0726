
## Summary Post

My initial post argued that the growth of agent-based systems is driven by ubiquity, interconnection, intelligence, delegation, and human orientation, with delegation particularly important because agents can act independently while pursuing organizational goals (Wooldridge and Jennings, 1995). The subsequent discussion helped me refine this position by showing that autonomy creates both opportunities and responsibilities.

My response to Abdullah highlighted that effective agents require more than autonomy. Proactivity, reactivity, and social ability are also important, while interactions between multiple agents may create coordination difficulties, conflicting goals, and unexpected emergent behaviour (Jennings, 2000). This showed me that autonomy, coordination, and governance must be designed together.

My exchange with Aldana developed this further by focusing on boundaries of delegation. I concluded that autonomy should be graduated according to risk. Routine and reversible decisions may be delegated, whereas decisions with serious financial, ethical, safety, or security consequences should retain human approval, monitoring, and override mechanisms.

My response to Khadijah extended the discussion from technical design to organizational impact. Rather than treating agents simply as replacements for employees, organizations should consider how tasks are distributed between humans and agents according to their respective capabilities. Russell and Norvig (2021, Chapter 8) reinforce the importance of how knowledge and relationships are represented when intelligent systems reason about their environment.

Unit 3 further strengthened my understanding of agent architecture. The lecturecast shows how control loops connect percepts, domain knowledge, and actions, while intentions create commitment but may also produce unintended side effects. Subsumption architecture demonstrates how simple behavioural layers can interact to produce complex emergent behaviour (University of Essex Online, 2026; Brooks, 1991).

Overall, my view has evolved from seeing autonomy mainly as an organizational advantage to understanding that successful agent-based systems require appropriate architecture, bounded delegation, human oversight, coordination, and accountability throughout their operational lifecycle.
