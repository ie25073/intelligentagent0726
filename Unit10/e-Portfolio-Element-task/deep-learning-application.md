# Deep Learning Application: Generative AI for Academic Research

## Technology overview

Generative large language models are a deep-learning application with substantial potential to affect education and academic research. Systems such as ChatGPT can respond to research questions, explain concepts, reformulate queries, summarise text and assist with drafting. In an agent-based system, a language model can also act as a planner that converts a high-level goal into a sequence of searches or other tool calls. These capabilities could make early-stage literature exploration faster and more accessible, particularly when researchers are unfamiliar with the terminology of a field.

## How it works

A large language model learns statistical patterns from large collections of text. A transformer architecture uses attention to model relationships between tokens and predicts a likely continuation from the supplied context. During generation, the model repeatedly selects tokens to construct an answer. This process can produce fluent and contextually useful text, but it does not itself retrieve evidence or verify truth. A research agent can reduce this limitation by combining the model with external scholarly search. In my Academic Research Agent, an optional local model proposes bounded search tasks, OpenAlex retrieves publication metadata, and deterministic code validates, deduplicates and ranks the returned records. Source identifiers and an execution trace support later checking.

## Socio-technical impact

The positive impact could include faster query formulation, improved access to specialist vocabulary and support for organising large numbers of candidate sources. However, efficiency can create over-reliance. Walters and Wilder (2023) found fabricated citations in literature reviews produced by the models they evaluated. Their historical figures cannot be generalised to every current model or prompt, but they demonstrate that fluent output is not reliable proof of a publication's existence. An invented citation may waste research time, distort a literature review and undermine academic integrity.

Bias is another concern. Training data and scholarly indexes reflect unequal visibility across languages, regions, disciplines and publication venues. Automated ranking can make these imbalances less visible while reinforcing them. Privacy and intellectual-property concerns may also arise if users submit unpublished work or personal data to hosted models. NIST (2024) therefore treats confabulation, harmful bias, privacy, information integrity and human over-reliance as risks that require governance across the AI lifecycle.

I would address these risks by separating planning from evidence selection, retaining DOI and source metadata, and requiring human approval before research output is disseminated. Model responses should be schema-validated, searches and retries should have explicit limits, and unavailable evidence should trigger abstention rather than invention. Evaluation should use manually checked questions and measure citation validity, relevance, claim support and appropriate abstention across topics and languages. These controls reduce rather than eliminate risk, so limitations and AI assistance must remain visible to users.
