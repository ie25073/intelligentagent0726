# Initial Post

Generative AI systems such as DALL-E and ChatGPT raise ethical concerns because convincing content can influence people without being accurate, fair or consensually produced. Their creative and educational benefits are valuable, but these benefits do not remove responsibility for foreseeable harm.

First, generated prose can misrepresent evidence. Walters and Wilder (2023) examined 636 references in literature reviews generated using GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 and found fabricated citations in both models. These historical results should not be treated as error rates for today's systems, but they demonstrate why fluent writing cannot establish factual reliability. In an academic research agent, an invented reference could distort a literature review and undermine the researcher's integrity.

Second, generated images and text can reproduce stereotypes or marginalise underrepresented groups. NIST (2024) identifies harmful bias, privacy risks and information integrity as significant concerns. For example, repeatedly depicting leaders as men could reinforce assumptions about who belongs in positions of authority. Synthetic impersonation also raises questions about consent and reputational harm, while the reuse of creative work raises ethical questions about attribution and fair treatment of creators.

The World Health Organization (2021) emphasises autonomy, transparency, accountability and equity. Although its guidance concerns healthcare, I consider these principles useful for evaluating generative systems more broadly. Responsibility should remain identifiable across developers, deployers and users, with practical routes for challenging harmful outputs.

For our proposed Academic Research Agent, I would require traceable sources, checks that citations support each claim, disclosure of AI assistance and human approval before dissemination. I would also evaluate performance across topics and languages, minimise personal data collection and document limitations. These safeguards require time and may reduce automation, but that trade-off is justified where unsupported outputs could mislead others.

How could we make human review sufficiently rigorous without allowing it to become a routine endorsement of whatever the system generates?
