## Summary post

Across Units 9 to 11, my understanding of generative AI has shifted from seeing ethical concerns mainly as output problems to recognising them as lifecycle and sociotechnical risks. In my initial post, I focused on reliability, bias, consent and authorship, arguing that convincing Deep Learning outputs do not guarantee factual accuracy. Walters and Wilder (2023) show that large language models can generate fabricated academic citations, while NIST (2024) identifies confabulation, harmful bias, privacy, information integrity and over-reliance as important generative-AI risks.

The peer discussion developed this further. Frederick Ogunlesi’s response reinforced source verification, consistent with Mugaanyi et al. (2024), and raised the value of diverse feedback loops. I concluded that such feedback should be combined with representative evaluation data, subgroup testing and independent review. Panagiotis Monogios broadened the discussion to privacy, intellectual property, deepfakes and model memorisation, concerns demonstrated by Carlini et al. (2023). His sociotechnical framing strengthened my view that safeguards should cover data provenance, consent, output monitoring and accountability rather than focus only on the model.

Unit 9’s Deep Learning material explains the technical basis for these concerns: models learn complex patterns from large datasets and can generate new text or images from learned representations. Unit 10’s peer-review activity showed why risk controls should be challenged collaboratively rather than accepted at face value. Unit 11 demonstrated intelligent agents in action across Industry 4.0, digital twins and financial modelling. Wang et al. (2016) show how autonomous smart objects can cooperate and coordinate through shared information, while the lecturecast illustrates applications in fraud detection and production optimisation.

Overall, I now see responsible intelligent-agent deployment as balancing capability with governance. Effective practice requires verification, provenance, representative testing, continuous monitoring, human oversight and proportionate controls based on the potential impact of failure. These controls should evolve as agents, contexts and risks change over time.
